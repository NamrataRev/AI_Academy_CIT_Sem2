# Comparing Approaches

---

Three countries. Three different responses to the same challenge.

AI is developing rapidly everywhere. The risks are real everywhere. The need for responsible practice is universal. But the EU, the US, and India have each responded in ways shaped by their own values, legal traditions, economic priorities, and political contexts.

Understanding the differences is not just academic. As someone who will build AI systems professionally — possibly deployed across borders, possibly for multinational companies, possibly in a regulatory landscape that does not yet exist in its final form — you need to understand how the approaches fit together.

---

## The Three Approaches Side by Side

| | EU AI Act | White House EO | India MeitY |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Type** | Binding law | Presidential directive | Advisory and principles |
| **Legal weight** | High — fines, market exclusion | Medium — agency compliance | Low — voluntary for most |
| **Approach** | Risk-based categorisation | Values-based themes | Principles-based guidance |
| **Scope** | Any system deployed in the EU | US federal agencies + industry guidance | Indian market and public services |
| **Enforcement** | Fines up to €35M or 7% global revenue | Agency compliance requirements | Limited formal enforcement |
| **Pace** | Slow and deliberate — years in development | Fast — executive action | Evolving rapidly |
| **Innovation stance** | Cautious — protect first, innovate within rules | Balanced — safety and competitiveness | Pro-innovation — guide rather than restrict |
| **Specific to AI type** | Yes — general purpose AI has specific rules | Focused on most powerful models | Sector-focused (healthcare, education, agriculture) |

---

## What They Share

Despite the differences, three themes appear across all three approaches. These are the emerging global consensus on responsible AI.

**Transparency** — users should know when they are interacting with AI and what that AI is doing. EU requires disclosure for limited-risk systems. EO required watermarking of AI-generated content. MeitY advisory required labelling. The mechanism differs. The principle is universal.

**Safety testing before deployment** — systems should be tested for risks before they reach users. EU requires conformity assessment for high-risk systems. EO required red-teaming for high-capability models. MeitY emphasises safety and trustworthiness. The threshold and formality differ. The expectation is the same.

**Human oversight for high-stakes decisions** — AI should not make consequential decisions about people's lives autonomously. EU mandates human oversight for high-risk systems. EO emphasised human oversight in federal AI use. MeitY guidance on public services emphasises accountability. All three say: for decisions that significantly affect people, humans must remain in the loop.

---

## Where They Differ — and Why It Matters

**Binding vs advisory**

The EU AI Act creates legal obligations. Non-compliance has financial consequences and can result in being excluded from the EU market. MeitY guidelines are advisory — non-compliance has no formal penalty for most actors. The EO's legal force depends on political continuity.

Why it matters: If you build a system deployed in the EU, compliance is not optional. If you build a system deployed only in India, the regulatory pressure is currently lower — but the ethical obligations are the same, and the regulatory landscape is changing.

**Risk-based vs values-based**

The EU starts with: what kind of system is this? The answer determines the obligations. The EO started with: what values must be upheld? The answer determines the themes. India starts with: what sectors are a priority? The answer determines where attention is focused.

Why it matters: A system that is minimal-risk under the EU framework may still violate the EO's non-discrimination requirements if it produces biased outputs. Compliance with one framework does not guarantee alignment with another. Good governance means thinking about all three — what type of system is this, what values does it uphold, and who does it affect.

**Global reach vs domestic focus**

The EU AI Act has extraterritorial reach — it applies to any system used in the EU, regardless of where it was built. The EO applies to US federal agencies and the most powerful AI developers. MeitY focuses on the Indian market.

Why it matters: A system built in India and deployed globally may need to comply with EU requirements. A system built in the US and sold to European customers must meet EU AI Act obligations. The regulatory landscape is not one jurisdiction — it is all of them simultaneously, for any system deployed across borders.

---

## The Practical Takeaway

You will build systems in a world where multiple regulatory frameworks apply simultaneously, where the rules are still being written, and where good governance matters regardless of what any specific regulation requires.

The practical position: build to the highest standard you will encounter, not the lowest. If your system will ever be used in the EU — build for EU compliance. If it affects people in India — evaluate across India's diversity. If it makes decisions that affect anyone's rights or access to services — apply human oversight.

The three frameworks converge on the same practices. Transparency. Testing. Human oversight. Documentation. These are not EU requirements or US requirements or Indian requirements. They are good engineering, recognised globally.

---

## Quick Recap

- EU AI Act: binding law, risk-based, broad scope, strong enforcement, cautious on innovation
- White House EO: presidential directive (partially revoked), values-based, focused on powerful models and federal agencies, balanced stance
- India MeitY: advisory principles, sector-focused, limited enforcement, pro-innovation
- What they share: transparency, safety testing before deployment, human oversight for high-stakes decisions
- Where they differ: binding force, risk-based vs values-based vs sector-based framing, global vs domestic reach
- Practical takeaway: build to the highest standard you will encounter — good governance practices are recognised across all three frameworks
