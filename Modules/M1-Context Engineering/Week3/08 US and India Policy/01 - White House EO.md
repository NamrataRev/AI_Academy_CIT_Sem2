# White House Executive Order on AI

---

Two neighbours build fences around their properties.

One neighbour measures carefully, files permits, follows the local code, and installs a fence that meets every specification. The process is slow and documented.

The other neighbour decides on a design, talks to the people affected, agrees on standards informally, and builds quickly. The outcome is good — but the process was different.

The EU AI Act and the White House Executive Order on AI are these two neighbours. Same goal — responsible AI development. Very different approaches to getting there.

---

## What the Executive Order Is

On October 30, 2023, President Biden signed the Executive Order on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence — the most significant US government action on AI to date.

An executive order is a directive from the President to federal agencies. It does not require congressional approval and it does not have the same legal weight as a law passed by Congress. But it carries real authority — federal agencies must comply, and it shapes how the US government approaches AI across all its functions.

The EO has since been partially modified — the Trump administration revoked parts of it in early 2025. But its core themes remain influential in how US industry and government approach AI governance, and the frameworks it established continue to shape practice.

---

## The Core Themes

The White House EO organised its requirements around eight key areas. Four are most relevant for what you are building:

**Safety and Security Testing**

For the most powerful AI models — those above a certain capability threshold — developers must share safety test results with the US government before public release. Red-teaming (deliberate adversarial testing) is required. The EO established that the government has a right to know about risks before they reach the public.

This is the principle you applied when you ran adversarial inputs against your context package — deliberately trying to break it before users encountered the failures. The EO formalised this practice as a requirement for high-capability models.

**Transparency and Watermarking**

AI-generated content should be identifiable as such. The EO directed the development of standards for watermarking AI-generated audio, video, and images. The goal: people should be able to know when content was made by a machine, not a human.

This connects directly to the EU AI Act's transparency obligations for limited-risk systems. Different mechanism, same principle.

**Civil Rights and Algorithmic Discrimination**

The EO placed significant emphasis on ensuring AI systems do not discriminate based on race, gender, disability, or other protected characteristics. It directed agencies to evaluate how AI used in housing, healthcare, employment, and criminal justice affects different groups.

For any system that makes decisions affecting people — even informally — this means actively thinking about who might be disadvantaged by the system's errors and how.

**Workforce and Education**

The EO directed investment in AI literacy and education — preparing workers for an economy where AI is widespread. The explicit acknowledgment: AI will change what skills are valuable, and education must respond.

This is directly relevant to you. The skills you are building — context engineering, evaluation, governance, backend integration — are the skills the EO identified as important for an AI-integrated economy.

## How the EO Differs From the EU AI Act

| | EU AI Act | White House EO |
|---|---|---|
| **Type** | Binding law | Presidential directive (partially revoked) |
| **Scope** | Any AI system deployed in the EU | US federal agencies and guidance to industry |
| **Approach** | Risk categorisation — different rules for different risk levels | Thematic — safety, transparency, civil rights, workforce |
| **Enforcement** | Fines and market access restrictions | Agency compliance, industry guidance |
| **Timeline** | Phased implementation 2024-2026 | Immediate agency action (some provisions revoked 2025) |
| **Focus** | Who is affected and at what risk level | What values AI development must uphold |

The EU approach asks: what kind of system is this, and what does that require?
The US approach asks: what values must AI development uphold, and how do we ensure they are respected?

Same destination. Different maps.

## What This Means for You

You are unlikely to be directly subject to the White House EO — it applies to federal agencies and to developers of the most powerful AI models, not to students building course projects.

But the themes it established are the themes you will work within professionally:

- Red-teaming and adversarial testing before deployment
- Transparency about what AI systems are and what they do
- Awareness of how systems affect different groups differently
- AI literacy as a professional competency

These are not abstract policy concerns. They are engineering practices that appear in job descriptions, in company governance policies, and in client requirements. Understanding where they came from makes you a more informed practitioner.

## Quick Recap

- The White House EO on AI (October 2023) directed federal agencies on safe, secure, and trustworthy AI development — partially revoked in 2025 but still influential
- Core themes: safety testing and red-teaming, transparency and watermarking, civil rights and non-discrimination, workforce and education
- The EU AI Act is binding law with risk-based categorisation; the EO is a presidential directive organised around values
- The practices the EO required — adversarial testing, transparency, bias awareness — appear in professional AI engineering regardless of which regulatory regime applies
